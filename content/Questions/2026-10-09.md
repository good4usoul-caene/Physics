The equation of the speed of light can be obtained by a combination of Faraday's Law and Ampere's Law (in the absence of a current):
$$\nabla \times \vec E =-\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}= -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial \vec E}{\partial t}\right)$$
The solutions of this equation are electrical waves, $E(x,y,z,t)$ whose phase velocity is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}$.  If a Lorentz Transformation is applied, the electrical field should be found to vary.  As an example, a long straight current-carrying wire is found to have no electrical field in its rest-frame.  But a boost along the direction of its length causes either contraction or un-contraction of the electrons, while causing contraction of the protons.  This difference in contraction results in the electrical field.  But the values of $\mu_0$ and $\varepsilon_0$ should remain the same.  

As for why you're saying $\mu_0$ and $\varepsilon_0$ are covariant, it might be the case that an analysis of solenoids and other "non-inertial" distributions of current might transform in other ways.  But the rule of Special Relativity is that the laws of physics are upheld in inertial frames, not arbitrary coordinate systems.  So I should expect that $\mu_0$ and $\epsilon_0$ be the same in a vacuum for all inertial observers.
