## Motivating Question:
Given some arc-length, $s$ of a circle of radius $r$ it is popular to calculate an angle $\theta = \frac{s}{r}$ and then either say that the quantity is "unitless" because the ratio of distance/distance cancels, or to say that the quantity has units of "radians".  

>[!info]- Which is correct?
>
>Neither claim is incorrect.  Mathematical forms such as sine, cosine, tangent will only accept a unitless variable as their input.  They can also accept radians.
>

>[!info]- What about degrees?
>
>Degrees multiply radians by a conversion factor of 
>
>$$\theta_\text{degree}=\frac{180 \theta_{radian}}{\pi}$$
>
>And the sine, cosine, and tangent functions can convert to a mode that accepts degrees.

>[!info]- Area Ratio
>
>This same angle may be described as an area ratio:  The area swept out by the arc $\Delta s$ as compared to the area of the full circle is also $\theta$, having units of $m^2/m^2$
## More to say about unitlessness

[[Numbers are Adjectives]] and even unitless quantities in physics nearly always describe something.  For instance, in the case of radians, the unit is $$\text{radians}= \Tfrac{meters of arc}{meter of radius}=\Tfrac{square meters of arc}{square meters of circle}$$
