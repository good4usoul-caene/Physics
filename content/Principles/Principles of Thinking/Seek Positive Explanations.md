A common practice in communication is to make negative statements, such as "of course the star did not *really* move".  One should take care, though, to make sure that the misconception being disputed is carefully defined, and that it is contrasted with a very clear positive explanation.

It is easy to think of only one interpretation of some phrase and call it a "misconception", while other interpretations of the same phrase might be perfectly valid.  

# Examples
- Of course the star did not "really move".  [[Aberration]]
	  - This can depend on context.  "Really moving" for instance, can refer to a world-line being caused to curve do to an influence of force, or refer to a world-line changing its slope under Lorentz or Galilean Transformation.  
- The universe cannot be described without a point-of-view.
	- There are examples of problems in math that may not invoke any relation to spatial coordinates.  For instance, an equation might describe the population of predators and prey over time.  While time is a coordinate, the populations are not coordinates.    But any visual imagining of the universe (what we see in pictures or film) requires a [[Point-of-View]].   See also:  [[Ptolemaic vs Copernican]].
	- An associated positive statement:  **All mathematical relations can be plotted.**  Even in the case of population vs. time, one can create a visual representation in space and time, by labeling one axis "population" and the other axis "year".   Or by labeling one axis "predators" and the other axis "prey" and allowing the point to animate over time.  In order to *look* at this plot, it is best to locate your point-of-view in such a way that the plot itself is *stationary*, and in *front* of your face.  See [[Euclidean Points and Parallel World Lines]]
