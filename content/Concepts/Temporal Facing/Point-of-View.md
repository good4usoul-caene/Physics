
An ideal eye or camera is a ==point of view==.  The eye or camera may be moved around, so it's [[world-line]] is not necessarily straight.  However at any given moment it's motion is well defined.  One way of thinking of the camera or eye is as an [[1st and 3rd Person|Avatar]] or a "figure representing a particular person" in reality. 

In order to obtain the coordinates of events relevant to the eye or camera, one must perform the [[Lorentz Transformation]] that renders the camera's current velocity equal to 0.  With this in mind, the correct perspectival distances and times are given at the origin, when
 - **Here:**  ($x'=0$) The spatial coordinate of the camera from the camera's point-of-view is "here", which puts the camera at the origin.
 - **Now:** (t'=0) The temporal coordinate of the camera from the camera's point-of-view is "now", which puts the camera at the origin.
 - **Stationary**' $v'=0$ The velocity of the camera from the camera's point-of-view is zero.  Even if the camera may be under acceleration, it's current velocity is zero.  
### The Logic

 - There are some who argue that a point-of-view under acceleration ought to observe something different than one who occupies the same position at the same time as an inertial observer with the same velocity.  As for ==what difference it makes== though, they tend to fall silent.  
 - **What would be different?**  An accelerating point-of-view would see something different immediately before and immediately after the "here and now" event.  
 - **What else would be different?**  An accelerating point-of-view would experience certain stresses that an inertial observer wouldn't experience.  Large stresses might warp a camera lens.  A non-ideal camera might change velocities between the time the light entered the lens to the time that it registers on the photosensitive plate.
 - **What would NOT be different**  The instantaneous geometry of the situation is the same for the accelerating point-of-view and the inertial point-of-view.  Except for internal forces that would mangle the camera, the observations of the global coordinates ==must== be the same.
 - **Conclusion:** Observation of distant events is wholly determined by instantaneous position, instantaneous time, and instantaneous velocity of the point-of-view.  It is not a function of acceleration.  All observers sharing the same point at the same time having the same velocity observe the same coordinates for all events.
