If you're asking me if the Minkowski metric itself has a great deal of explanatory power, I would say, no--not for me. For me, the Pythagorean Theorem is not terribly intuitive, but I've seen the math that convinces me that it's an invariant measure. What *is* important to me, though, is to have an operative description of what those measures mean.

Let's start with a more elementary question.

If someone drives for an hour from point A to point B at 60 miles per hour, I'm sure your inclination is to say that the distance is 60 miles. And in all fairness, the distance between those two points *is* 60 miles.

- [ ] Hurdle 1: Does it make sense to you that the "proper distance" between **events** A and B is zero, even though the distance between the **points** A and B is nonzero?  Also see [[Proper Distance vs Length Contraction]].

However, what if, instead of the two stationary points, we were asked to find the proper distance as measured by a ruler tracking the moving point. That is, what is the measure between event A and event B as measured by a ruler in the vehicle that is traversing the distance between the departure event and the arrival event?

Is it fair to say that the "proper" distance between the two events is zero? If you would say "no" then the issue may be psychological. Somehow you are disinclined to accept the measure of moving rulers as a measure of proper distance.

- [ ] Does it make sense that the traveler gauges the distance between points A and B as a time instead of a distance?

If you would say "yes" then we can move on to "how, then, is the distance between these two events described, by persons traveling in the car, who adopt the view that they are stationary and the world is moving past them?" Since there is no distance between events A and B, the interval between them is entirely represented by time.

So the first psychological hurdle is saying "For any two events where a ruler can travel between them so they both happen at the same measure, the proper distance is zero".

The second psychological hurdle may be about Stellar [[Aberration]]  If you're familiar with James Bradley's discovery, and if you have embraced the truth about it without any cognitive dissonance, then the question is "where was the star when it emitted the light we're seeing now." There are ways to describe the answer to this question with rainfall analogies that are helpful, but they don't get to the fundamental issue: the event moved. The location where the star was when it emitted the light moves around, not under the influence of any forces, but due to the transformation of our coordinate system into a reference frame where the star was coming from a different direction.

- [ ] Hurdle 2:  Does it make sense that the location where the star-light emitted from shifts as we change velocities?

I would say the third idea that ought be introduced is the idea of a light-wave front. Imagine that a distant star emits a solar flare, and the solar flare is observed at events C and D. Notably, these two events don't meet the requirement of the previous situation. No ruler can travel fast enough to mark both of these events at the same position at different times. But instead, we can ask, "what velocity would be needed to observe both of these events at the same time"?

For reference, though, if $$
\frac{\Delta x}{c\Delta t}=\frac{1}{\beta}
$$, where $$
\beta<1
$$
 then one must choose a velocity of $v=\beta c$. This should put the emission of the flare somewhere in the plane bisecting the events.

# The formula

The "space-time interval" between two events is, mathematically, an invariant quantity, but it is different from coordinate time or the Euclidean Distance, which are affected by the observer's speed.

Here's how the invariant Minkowski metric ought to be compared to time and Euclidean distance:

  

For a time-like interval, there exists some frame of reference where the two events occur at the same place, and the time between them from that point-of-view is 

$$
\tau=\frac{\sqrt{ct^2-x^2-y^2-z^2}}{c}
$$

  

For a spacelike interval, there exists some frame of reference where the the two events occur at the same time, and the distance between the two events from that point-of-view is 

$$
\chi=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2-ct^2}
$$



For a speed-of-light interval, there exist an infinity of frames of reference where the time and distance between the emission and absorption of the photon is negligible. If you accelerate along the vector from the source to the target, the time and Euclidean distance is reduced (by Bondi's k-factor). If you accelerate the other way, it is increased (by Bondi's k-factor)

# Unfamiliarity or Disdain

Does everything above seem reasonable?

The question is whether you are not familiar with the idea, or if you are familiar, but you view the idea with disdain. If you're already familiar with the description that I just gave, then it may be a psychological or philosophical problem... not because it is unfamiliar, but because it seems insane.

For instance, Voyager 1 is now 172 Astronomical Units from the Sun. And so if I asked "How far has Voyager 1 traveled since it launched from Earth." you would probably be inclined to say "around 172 AU'. That's not an unreasonable answer, but what if I said, "Properly, it has not gone anywhere, but rather, the sun and earth have moved away from it"? If you *can* entertain that idea, that the actual "proper" distance traveled by Voyager 1 is no distance at all, then you are equipped to psychologically and philosophically understand the Minkowski metric. If, on the other hand, you view the idea with disdain, or as insane, it is possible that you are psychologically incompatible with the Minkowski metric, as a measure of "proper" time or "proper" distance.
