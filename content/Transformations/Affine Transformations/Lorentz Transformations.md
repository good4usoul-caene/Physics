The Lorentz Transformation accomplish the following expectations:
1. Take all objects whose velocity is $v<c$ and render them stationary
2. Take all objects whose velocity is 0, and render them moving at $-v$.
3. Take all objects (photons) with $v=\pm c$ and preserve their velocity.

### Compare to Galilean Transformation
The Lorentz Transformation accomplish the following expectations:
1. Same
2. Same
3. Preserve global synchronization
4. The transformation accomplishing these three things is called a "**Galilean Boost**"
### Inverse Transformation
  The inverse transformation is similar, except it takes stationary objects to velocity $v$ and renders objects at $-v$ stationary.

### Boost
Conceptually a **Boost** means we are imagining an observer who is changing velocity.  The forward transformation is used to find a [[Local and Global Mapping| Global Mapping]] from the original [[Inertial Reference Frame]] to the final reference frame.

### Instantaneous and Gradual Boost
A boost is generally imagined to be instantaneous, which means there is a sudden change in perspective.  But what is the actual distinction between an instantaneous and gradual boost?  We can imagine an instantaneous boost geometrically as a hyperbolic rotation around a single event in spacetime.  But as we imagine it, time is actually passing.  The event remains in place on the spacetime diagram.  All the events follow hyperbolas around it.  The lattice of spacetime skews along the $x=ct$ and $x=-ct$ axes.  Move away from the single event, and some of the hyperbolic paths can represent an extended accelerating body in the [[Rindler Coordinates]].

### Rapidity
The Lorentz Transformations are best expressed in terms of a quantity called **Rapidity** of the boost.  The rapidity of the boost is $$\eta=\operatorname{arctanh}(v/c)$$
Rapidity will be used extensively in the discussions on this site.  The reason for this is as simple as it is profound:

### Bondi k-factor
The Lorentz Transformations preserve the speed of light, so all $x=ct$ lines and $x=-ct$ lines retain their slope in the spacetime diagram.  If we have two photons going to specific points in space and time, from $(0,0)$ to $(x_l,t_l)$ and $(x_r,t_r)$, we can be sure that $x_l/t_l=-c$ and $x_r/t_r=c$ before the transformation.  We can also be sure that after the transformation,  $x_l'/t_l'=-c$ and $x_r'/t_r'=c$.  The speed of light is conserved.  But how?  Well, as it turns out both $x_l\rightarrow x_l'$ and $t_l\rightarrow t_l'$ either increase or decrease by the same factor.  That factor is $\exp(\pm \eta)$ Likewise, both $x_r\rightarrow x_r'$ and $t_r'\rightarrow t_r'$ decrease or increase by the reciprocal factor.  This factor is sometimes called the Bondi k-factor.
