Let's modernize the Einstein Synchronization convention a little bit.

We imagine a transmission and receiving station with cameras and emitters all around in a big sphere or dome. 

One big flash is emitted and called the zero event.  

Each clock reflects back the zero event.  The time on the clock at this point is not important.  What is important is that the receiving station can detect the signal, and notify THAT clock of the time that it got THAT signal.  In particular, since the time at the receiving station, $T$ is equal to the round-trip time, it should instruct that clock to set it's time based on the delay of another one-way trip.  So when it gets the signal reflecting the reflection, it should set its time to $1.5T$.  

The animation [Einstein Synchronization Convention | Desmos](https://www.desmos.com/calculator/6ysgrbngci) shows the original signal going out as purple round dots.  The reflection signal comes back in the form of faded blue rectangles, and the central station in the demo goes ahead and multiplies it's time by 1.5 so that each clock sets its time to the value sent, rather than having to do an extra calculation.
