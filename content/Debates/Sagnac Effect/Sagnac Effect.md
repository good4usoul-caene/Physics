The Sagnac Effect is understood in two main examples:
	1. When your cell phone picks up GPS signals from satellites or ground stations, it has traveled a little bit to the east since the signals were sent.  The amount it traveled to the east is based on the rotation of the earth, and it depends on your latitude.  A correction formula is used to account for the earliness in getting west-bound signals, and the delay in getting east-bound signals.  Your GPS device handles all of this automatically, and so the phenomenon is largely invisible to the user of the GPS device.
	2. The Sagnac Interferometer is a fast-spinning disk equipped with mirrors, a beam splitter, a laser, and an interferometer.  The signal comes out from the laser, then goes into the **beam splitter**, where half the light goes clockwise, and the other half goes clounterclockwise.  When the beam goes all the way around and then returns to the beam splitter at the end, the light recombines and enters an interferometer.  If there is no interference pattern.  **The question** that the interferometer asks is "If the disk is spinning, is the non-inertial nature of the device apparent from the interference pattern?"  **The answer** is that yes, indeed, there is an interference pattern.  


### The Conundrum

Analyzing [[Spin Local Coordinates]] we find that there is nothing "strasnge" about the local coordinates of the Sagnac Scenario.  The speed of light is the same both to the left and to the right.  Locally, for all intents and purposes, one has no explanation for the interference pattern.  No observer sees an isotropy in the speed of light, all the way around the circle.  So how then, is it possible that the total time necessary for the light to travel around is both **longer** in one direction and the other, and is also **the same** in both directions.

### The Choice

Observers on the rim have two main choices for how to set and synchronize their clocks.  
1. One way to do it is to watch for a signal from the axle.  If they watch for the signal from the axle and then adjust for the radius from the axle, they can all sync their clocks with the axle clock.  The problem with doing this is that when they do, they will not find their clocks in sync with each other.  In fact, the amount by which they are out of sync is exactly the amount needed to account for the interference pattern.
2. Another way to do it is for one of the rim observers to take his left clock, and sync his right clock to be the same.  Then proceeding around to the right, the next observer syncs his right clock to be the same.  Each observer does this until the procedure comes back around to where it started.  However, much to his dismay, the first observer finds that the last observer wants to set his left clock to a new time.
3. If a "road" or "conveyor belt" is available it might be useful to sync all of the clocks on the conveyor belt, and then as the rim rolls along the conveyor, each rim observer simply takes the time of the conveyor belt.  Two visualizations of this are [Sagnac With Linear Sync Convention | Desmos](https://www.desmos.com/calculator/fz1jylfqwn) and [Rel-Roll-Wheel | Desmos](https://www.desmos.com/calculator/670c6e9ed0)  The first visualization imagines from the inertial frame of the axle.  The conveyor belts all pass at the same velocity as the rim, but their clocks are out of sync due to relativistic effects.  What ends up happening is that the clock at the **beam splitter** ends up having two different times, or more.  Each time the beam splitter touches the road/conveyor, it inherits a new time from the same inertial frame.
